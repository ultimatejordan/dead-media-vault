Host: Maria Sibylla Merian was born in Frankfurt in sixteen forty-seven, into a family of printmakers. Her father died when she was three. Her stepfather, the still-life painter Jacob Marrel, taught her to paint flowers, and by her teens she had a second obsession: insects.

In sixteen seventy-nine she published her findings as a book of engravings. She was thirty-two.

Host: In eighteen fifty-five, the American architect Richard Morris Hunt was in Paris. He found, in some shop, a small copper globe — about five inches across, hollow, two halves joined at the equator with a wire through the poles. Nobody knew what it was. Nobody cared. Hunt bought it, quote, "for a song," brought it home, and let his children play with it.

Host: Years later, dining with Hunt in eighteen seventy, a bookseller named Henry Stevens saw the thing sitting around and recognized it for what it was: one of the oldest globes on Earth. Made around fifteen ten. Only the second globe ever known to depict the Americas. He told Hunt to stop letting the children use a five-hundred-year-old artifact as a toy and store it in the Lenox Library, which Hunt was designing.

Host: Hunt handed it to James Lenox, the great bibliophile. Lenox's collection became the foundation of the New York Public Library. The globe sits in the Rare Book Division to this day.

Host: And on the eastern coast of Asia, just below the equator, in tiny engraved letters, it reads: "HIC SVNT DRACONES." Here are dragons.

Host: It is the only historical map in the world that says that. The only one. Every fantasy novel cover, every video game loading screen, every tattoo — the whole "here be dragons" mythology — traces back to this one copper ball that an architect bought cheap in Paris and gave to his kids as a plaything.

Host: And here's the part nobody tells you. When the first scholar to describe it, B. F. da Costa, wrote about the globe in eighteen seventy-nine, he didn't think it meant dragons at all. He argued it referred to the Dagroians — a people described by Marco Polo in his travels, who, according to Polo, feasted upon the dead and picked their bones.

Archive: "In this region, near the equatorial line, is seen 'Hc Svnt Dracones' — or, here are the Dagroians, described by Marco Polo as living in the kingdom of Dagroian."
