# Ukiyo-e Beyond Hokusai: 5 Masters Worth Knowing

*Target keyword: ukiyo-e prints — Brand: Gilt & Grotesque: Vintage Design Archive*

Everyone knows the Wave. Katsushika Hokusai's *Great Wave off Kanagawa* — that clawing blue crest with Mount Fuji sitting calm in the distance — is on phone cases, shower curtains, and at least one airline livery. It's a great print. It was made around 1831 as part of the *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji* (which ran to 46 prints because it sold so well they kept adding more). Hokusai lived to 88, changed his name some thirty times, and claimed that everything he'd done before seventy was worthless.

But here's the thing: the Wave is the *Mona Lisa* problem. One image got so famous it eclipsed an entire art form. Ukiyo-e — "pictures of the floating world" — ran for two centuries and produced thousands of masterpieces by dozens of artists. If your entire relationship with Japanese woodblock prints is the Wave and maybe a Hiroshige landscape on a tote bag, you're standing in the lobby of a very large building. Here are five rooms worth walking into.

## 1. Kitagawa Utamaro (1753–1806): the painter of women

Before Hokusai was famous, Utamaro owned the 1790s. He specialized in *bijinga* — "pictures of beautiful women" — and he painted the women of Edo's pleasure quarters and teahouses with an intimacy nobody had attempted in print before: close-up head studies, women nursing children, women caught in unguarded moments. His lines are impossibly fine, his patterns obsessive.

Utamaro's career ended the way careers ended under the shogunate: in 1804 he was arrested and briefly imprisoned for making prints of the disgraced warlord Toyotomi Hideyoshi — a political crime. He died two years later, at fifty-three. His *bijinga* defined the genre so completely that every "beautiful woman" print made after him is in dialogue with his work, whether the artist knew it or not.

**Look for:** the *ōkubi-e* (large-head pictures) — faces filling the whole sheet, printed with mica dust backgrounds that shimmer.

## 2. Tōshūsai Sharaku: the ghost (active 1794–1795)

Sharaku is the great mystery of ukiyo-e. He appeared out of nowhere in 1794, produced around 145 prints — all of them portraits of kabuki actors, all of them startling — and vanished within ten months. Nobody knows who he was. Theories range from a Noh actor to a disgruntled samurai to a collective pseudonym. Two hundred thirty years of scholarship has not settled it.

The prints themselves are the strangest in the form: where every other *yakusha-e* (actor print) flattered its subject, Sharaku drew actors with exaggerated, almost grotesque expressions — bulging eyes, grimacing mouths, faces caught mid-performance like a photograph taken a second too late. Contemporary audiences reportedly found them unsettling. Modern audiences find them electric. They're the punk rock of ukiyo-e: confrontational, technically brilliant, and over before you could get used to them.

**Look for:** the bust portraits from the first printing period (mid-1794) — mica backgrounds, the actor's name cartouche, expressions that look like they were drawn by someone with a grudge.

## 3. Utagawa Hiroshige (1797–1858): the poet of the road

If Hokusai painted nature as drama, Hiroshige painted it as weather. His *Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō* (1833–1834) follows the great coastal highway between Edo and Kyoto, station by station — but the prints aren't really about the road. They're about rain at Shōno, snow at Kambara, dusk over the rice fields: atmosphere as subject matter.

Hiroshige came from a samurai-class firefighting family, which may be why his prints have that quality of someone who has stood outside in bad weather and paid attention. Van Gogh copied his compositions (the *Courtesan* after Hiroshige, the flowering plum tree). Monet collected him. When the West discovered Japanese prints in the 1860s–70s, Hiroshige was the revelation: proof that a landscape could be about a feeling rather than a place.

He's sometimes called the last great master of classical ukiyo-e, which is true in the sense that the floating world was already dissolving under him — the Meiji Restoration came a decade after his death.

**Look for:** the vertical-format rain and snow scenes, where the weather is rendered in diagonal baren-swept lines across the whole sheet.

## 4. Tsukioka Yoshitoshi (1839–1892): the last master

The Art Gallery of New South Wales calls Yoshitoshi "the last great master of the ukiyo-e genre," and his final series is the argument: *Tsuki hyakushi* — *One Hundred Aspects of the Moon* — one hundred ōban woodblock prints published in batches between 1885 and 1892 by Akiyama Buemon, each one a moonlit scene drawn from Japanese and Chinese history, legend, literature, and theater.

Yoshitoshi lived through the collapse of the world ukiyo-e depicted. He was born into the old order and died in the new Japan, and his work has the intensity of someone who knows the form is dying and is determined to take it as far as it can go in the time left. The Moon series mixes samurai and courtesans with ghosts and madmen; some prints are serene, some are genuinely disturbing. Contemporary collectors reportedly queued before dawn to buy each new design as it was released — and still found the editions sold out. A complete set sold at Bonhams in 2022 for over $69,000.

He died in 1892, three years after finishing. Everything he published is public domain.

**Look for:** the warrior prints (*Inaba Mountain Moon*, 1885 — Toyotomi Hideyoshi climbing a cliff face) and the quiet ones (*Cooling off at Shijō*, a teahouse girl by the river), which show his full range.

## 5. Kawase Hasui (1883–1957): the heir

Hasui isn't Edo-period ukiyo-e at all — he's *shin-hanga*, the "new prints" movement of the early 20th century, which revived the old collaborative workshop system (artist, carver, printer, publisher) for modern subjects. Working with the publisher Watanabe Shōzaburō from the late 1910s, Hasui designed around 620 prints over nearly forty years: snow falling on Tokyo temples, rain on coastal roads, dusk settling over harbors.

Hasui's prints are the direct heirs of Hiroshige — same attention to weather and light, same melancholy — but filtered through a Japan of electric lines and railways. In 1956 the Japanese government named him a Living National Treasure. He died the following year. His early work, published before 1929, is public domain in the US; later prints are still in copyright, so check dates.

**Look for:** the snow scenes. Nobody in the history of printmaking has drawn falling snow better. That's not hyperbole, it's consensus.

One thing worth understanding about all of this: ukiyo-e was never a solo act. Every print was a collaboration between four specialists — the artist who drew the design, the carver who cut a separate cherry-wood block for each color, the printer who rubbed each block onto the paper by hand, and the publisher who financed the whole thing and owned the blocks. When you look at a great print, you're looking at four people's best work landing in the same square of paper. The *shin-hanga* movement's whole point was to prove that system still worked in the 20th century. It did.

## Buying real prints (or honest reproductions)

A word on the market, because it's full of fog:

**Originals:** A first-edition Hokusai Wave costs more than a house. But later impressions of 19th-century prints — printed from the original blocks years or decades later — can be affordable, and they're still period objects. What matters: early vs. late impression (crisp key-block lines vs. worn ones), publisher seals and censor seals in the margins, and *washi* paper with the right texture. Standard ōban size is roughly 39 × 26 cm — if the "original" is A4, it's not.

**Reproductions:** Most of us are buying reproductions, and that's fine — but buy from the scan, not from the marketing. The best reproductions are made from museum scans: the Library of Congress, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Museum of Fine Arts Boston, and the British Museum all have deep, free, high-resolution ukiyo-e holdings. A reproduction printed from a 4000-pixel Met scan will always beat one printed from a Pinterest thumbnail, no matter what the listing promises.

**The public-domain line:** Everything published before 1930 is public domain in the US as of 2026. That covers Utamaro, Sharaku, Hokusai, Hiroshige, and Yoshitoshi completely, and Hasui's early work. You can print them, sell the prints, put them on products. The images belong to everyone now — which is exactly what the publishers who sold them for pennies on the streets of Edo would have found either delightful or horrifying, and I'm not sure which.
